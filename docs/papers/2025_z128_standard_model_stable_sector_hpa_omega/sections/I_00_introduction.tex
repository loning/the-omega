
\section{Introduction: from tick-only readout to stable sectors}
\label{sec:introduction}

The Standard Model (SM) organizes known non-gravitational interactions into the gauge structure
\[
SU(3)\times SU(2)\times U(1),
\]
with chiral matter content and experimentally established parity violation and CP violation; see, e.g., \cite{PeskinSchroeder1995,PDGRPP2024}.
Despite its predictive success, several structural features remain ``input-like'' at the microscopic explanatory level: the origin of chirality, the meaning of gauge redundancy, and the origin of small CP-odd invariants.

This paper adopts the HPA--$\Omega$ viewpoint: observability is not the presence of continuous fields \emph{a priori}, but the output of a finite-resolution protocol produced by unitary scanning and window projection (Section~\ref{sec:hpa_readout}).
In this view, a ``particle'' is first a \emph{stable readout type}, and ``forces'' arise from the constraints required to keep readout consistent across space.

The present paper is written to be closed under its declared input set (Table~\ref{tab:audit_facing_contract} and Appendix~\ref{app:inference_ledger}): all finite constructions at the minimal anchor $(m,n)=(6,3)$ are defined and proved here, and all quantitative closures are reproduced by deterministic scripts.
Companion manuscripts in the same \texttt{docs/papers} repository provide extended context and alternative presentations \cite{Ma2025HPA,Ma2025CAP,Ma2025CAPII,Ma2025HHU,Ma2025HPT,Ma2025PCG,Ma2025RF,Ma2025HPOT,Ma2025FoP}, but they are not required to follow the present paper's definitions, proofs, and audits.

For reader navigation, Table~\ref{tab:resolution_spectrum} provides the global map of the resolution axis under the minimal calibration.
In the tick-first spine used here, the only primitive physical input is \emph{time as tick} (scan iteration count); all physical-language notions (space, distance, velocity, gauge connections, mass/energy scales) are treated as derived protocol structures.
Section~\ref{sec:tick_calculus} records the corresponding dictionary and fixes the time-first dependency order used throughout.

\subsection{Axiom 0: the tick (sequential readout) as the only primitive input (interface)}
\label{subsec:axiom0_linearity}
\begin{axiom}[Readout sequentiality (interface)]\label{ax:readout_sequentiality}
The microscopic description is \emph{sequential}.
An observer couples to a single unitary scan order that produces a one-dimensional readout stream, and finite observability appears through window projection (Section~\ref{sec:hpa_readout}).
Multi-dimensional language enters only through a choice of \emph{addressing basis} that folds the scan order into a locality structure (Section~\ref{sec:hilbert_addressing}); throughout, we use ``space'' and ``spacetime'' only in this protocol-level addressing sense (Conventions).
\end{axiom}

\paragraph{Time, signal rate, and matching dictionaries.}
Under Axiom~\ref{ax:readout_sequentiality}, ``time'' is operationally the iteration count along the scan.
Any conversion from the dimensionless scan rate to physical units is therefore a matching-layer and units dictionary: once a locality basis is fixed, a constant step advance defines a maximal protocol signal rate, while the measured constant $c$ appears only after choosing a physical calibration.
We do not treat $c$ as a theorem-level output of the folding core.

\paragraph{Matter as recurrent patterns and mass as protocol cost.}
In a linear ontology, stable excitations are not points placed in an ontic background, but persistent patterns in the readout stream that are stable under the chosen channels.
On the physical identification layer, we adopt the interface identification that an observed mass scale corresponds to protocol overhead required to resolve and stabilize a pattern at a given resolution (e.g.\ increased local scan density, deeper matching shifts, or larger folding-degeneracy costs in the finite invariants).
This paper makes this identification auditable by a closed depth coordinate and a reproducible mass-spectrum template (Section~\ref{sec:mass_spectrum_closure}).

\begin{remark}[Static ontology vs.\ sequential readout (interface)]\label{rem:static_ontology}
Although the paper is written in the scan-first language of Axiom~\ref{ax:readout_sequentiality}, one may also adopt a complementary intuition: the scan order together with the chosen addressing dictionary can be regarded as a fixed (already-defined) structure, while ``time'' is the ordered traversal experienced by a coupled observer.
In this reading, dynamical episodes are a playback effect of sequential readout on a static protocol substrate (a block-universe style viewpoint), not the construction of that substrate.
We use this only as interface intuition; none of the theorem-level finite folding statements depends on it as a premise.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[A historical analogy: Wheeler's one-electron universe]\label{rem:wheeler_one_electron}
As a historical analogy, Wheeler suggested to Feynman that the identity of electrons might admit a radical kinematic explanation: a single electron world-line could thread the observed phenomenology, with positrons interpreted as the same object propagating backward in time in the spacetime picture.
We use this only as an analogy for the present linear-ontology interface: a sequential primitive can generate rich multi-body effective structure once it is read through an addressing basis and a matching dictionary, without promoting the analogy to a premise of the mathematical layer.
See, e.g., \cite{Feynman1949TheoryPositrons,Feynman1965NobelLecture}.
\end{remark}

The key inversion relative to conventional field-first narratives is methodological:
we treat the problem of ``what particles and forces exist'' as a problem of \emph{which readout types are stable under a fixed protocol}.
Finite windows induce symbolic compression; stability constraints induce further compression; and the effective ``spectrum'' is the surviving stable sector.
From this viewpoint, discreteness is not a postulate but a consequence of finite-resolution projection.

\paragraph{Stability and agency as dual interface viewpoints.}
The stable-sector viewpoint used in this paper has a natural control-theoretic dual: in open systems, long-lived low-entropy structure can be modeled as \emph{predictive active error correction} (AEC) that suppresses protocol-level mismatch relative to a passive baseline while paying dissipation and implementation costs \cite{AstromMurray2008FeedbackSystems,Landauer1961}.
Appendix~\ref{app:isomorphism_dictionary} records a protocol-level dictionary that aligns stable-sector objects (alphabets, stability channels, degeneracies) with AEC objects (mismatch certificates, feedback, and audit constraints) without mixing mathematical-layer premises with physical identification claims.

\subsection{Derived localization anchor: the minimal rigid-frame display budget (interface)}
\label{subsec:6dof_lock}
While Axiom~\ref{ax:readout_sequentiality} fixes only sequentiality, any physical identification must specify what it means to \emph{display} a localized object.
In the tick-first dictionary of Section~\ref{sec:tick_calculus}, locality is a derived addressing graph, and a ``localized rigid frame'' is an interface notion tied to coarse pose distinguishability.
Let $d$ denote the effective spatial dimension of this dictionary at scales where rigid frames are meaningful.
A local rigid frame carries $d$ translational degrees of freedom and $d(d-1)/2$ rotational degrees of freedom, hence
\[
\dim SE(d)=d+\frac{d(d-1)}{2}=\frac{d(d+1)}{2}.
\]
We now state explicitly what the ``bit-per-DoF'' language means in this paper.
The claim is \emph{not} that $m$ bits can encode a continuous pose in $SE(d)$ with arbitrary precision.
Rather, it is a minimal coarse-lock assumption at the protocol interface: a length-$m$ window yields at most $2^m$ distinct binary readout classes, and to display a local rigid frame the protocol must be able to distinguish at least two coarse bins along each independent parameter of $SE(d)$.
In this minimal two-bin-per-parameter convention, a sufficient single-window coarse-admissibility condition is
\[
2^m\;\ge\;2^{\dim SE(d)}
\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
m\;\ge\;\dim SE(d)=\frac{d(d+1)}{2}.
\]
More generally, if one asks to specify an element of a $k$-dimensional Lie group up to an $\varepsilon$-scale resolution, the required number of bits scales as $m\gtrsim k\log(1/\varepsilon)$ (metric-entropy / quantization scaling) \cite{GrayNeuhoff1998Quantization,CoverThomas2006}.
In the present paper we use only the minimal two-bin-per-parameter anchor, and we keep the rigid-frame lock on the physical identification layer.
\begin{remark}[What the 6-DoF ``lock'' does and does not assert]\label{rem:6dof_lock_scope}
The 6-DoF coarse-lock is an \emph{interface} convention, not a theorem about continuous pose estimation.
It does not claim that $m=6$ is sufficient to ``lock'' a pose in $SE(3)$ at any fixed accuracy; continuous pose locking at accuracy $\varepsilon$ requires a bit budget scaling as $m\gtrsim 6\log(1/\varepsilon)$ in the standard quantization sense \cite{GrayNeuhoff1998Quantization,CoverThomas2006}.
It also does not claim that $m\ge 6$ is necessary for localization under all possible protocols: multi-window temporal integration, analog readout features, or additional structure/constraints can reduce the effective information required at a given task.
The role of the lock in this paper is narrower: it singles out $m=6$ as the smallest \emph{single-window} binary anchor that can support a coarse rigid-frame display in a $3$D locality dictionary under the stated minimal convention.
In CAP language, once a $\mu\leftrightarrow m$ selection rule is fixed at the protocol layer (Corollary~\ref{ax:resolution_selection}), the protocol uses the smallest admissible anchor throughout the corresponding energy band; thus, under the $3$D coarse-lock dictionary, $m=6$ is the CAP-minimal effective resolution across the ordinary-energy band until an uplift threshold is crossed.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[CAP-minimal rigidity lock selects the 3D six-bit anchor (interface)]\label{prop:cap_dim_lock}
Within the balanced-coupling candidate family $m\in\{4,6,8,\dots\}$, the smallest window length that matches the rigid-frame count $m=\frac{d(d+1)}{2}$ for some integer $d\ge 1$ is $m=6$, realized uniquely at $d=3$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For $d=1,2$ one has $\frac{d(d+1)}{2}\in\{1,3\}$, which is not in the even family $\{4,6,8,\dots\}$.
For $d=3$ one has $\frac{d(d+1)}{2}=6$, which is in the family.
For $d\ge 4$ one has $\frac{d(d+1)}{2}\ge 10>6$, so no smaller match exists.
\end{proof}
In CAP language (Axiom~\ref{ax:cap}), $d=3$ is the unique dimension that achieves an \emph{exact} rigid-frame lock at the smallest balanced scale.
Empirically, the observed locality structure is three-dimensional to high precision on laboratory and astrophysical scales, providing an external consistency check.

In a $3$D locality dictionary, a local rigid frame is naturally modeled by the Euclidean group
\[
SE(3)\cong \RR^3\rtimes SO(3),
\]
as a semidirect product; see, e.g., \cite{HallLieGroups2015}.
It is specified by a position $x\in\RR^3$ and an orientation $R\in SO(3)$.
Hence the minimal kinematic description carries six independent parameters: three translational plus three rotational degrees of freedom.

We treat this as a geometric coarse-lock on the physical identification layer: a fundamental readout window supplies one bit per kinematic degree of freedom of a local frame.
In $3$D this locks the minimal window to six bits, $\Omega_6=\{0,1\}^6$.
This statement belongs to the physical identification layer: it motivates a preferred anchor scale but is not used as a premise for any theorem-level folding statement.

To make the geometric exclusivity explicit, compare the first few balanced candidates $m\in\{4,6,8,\dots\}$ against the rigid-frame degree count $\frac{d(d+1)}{2}$ \cite{HallLieGroups2015}:
in $2$D one has $3$ DoF, so the balanced code length $m=4$ overspecifies the kinematics ($4>3$); in $4$D one has $10$ DoF, so the next balanced length $m=8$ underspecifies it ($8<10$).
Only in $3$D does the balanced length $m=6$ match exactly ($6=3+3$), yielding a unique geometric lock for a $3$D locality dictionary.

Once a locality-preserving addressing basis is fixed (e.g.\ Hilbert addressing on a chosen readout screen), one may adopt a balanced coupling convention that matches the $m$-bit readout alphabet size $2^m$ with the number of sites at Hilbert order $n$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:balanced_coupling_equiv}).
Throughout this paper we use the classical $2$D Hilbert addressing (a square grid), so the matched site count is $4^n=(2^2)^n$ and the balanced relation takes the form $m=2n$; other addressing dimensions would change this arithmetic match (Remark~\ref{rem:balanced_coupling_dimension} and Remark~\ref{rem:why_2d_hilbert}).
Balanced coupling is used only to attach spatial diagnostics and is not a theorem-level necessity for the folding core (Remark~\ref{rem:balanced_coupling_convention}).
At the 6-DoF coarse-lock anchor $m=6$, the chosen $2$D screen then yields $n=3$ and $2^6=4^3=64$.

\subsubsection{The geometric vacuum and protocol rejection}
\label{subsubsec:geometric_vacuum}
The 6-DoF coarse-lock has an immediate consequence: in a $3$D locality dictionary, any readout pattern that is to be displayed as a localized object must supply enough independent information to fix a local position--orientation frame.
Under the minimal coarse-lock hypothesis stated above (two bins per independent parameter), a single-window coarse display requires at least $m\ge 6$ binary distinctions; in this sense $m=6$ is the minimal interface anchor for matter-like localization in the stated dictionary.

For $m<6$, stable symbolic types still exist at the mathematical layer---indeed the golden-mean admissible set has size $|X_m|=F_{m+2}$ (Section~\ref{subsec:phi_channel})---but they are sub-geometric for a $3$D readout: they underdetermine the kinematic frame and cannot be consistently assigned to a unique local site-and-frame configuration.
We therefore treat these modes as \emph{protocol-rejected as matter}: they do not appear as stable localized particles, but persist as a non-local background that can seed transient fluctuations when coupled to higher-resolution readout.

The near-threshold case $m=5$ is illustrative.
It supports $|X_5|=F_7=13$ admissible types, and under the minimal staircase calibration used later (Section~\ref{subsec:p3_resolution_jumps}) its threshold scale lies at $\mu_{\mathrm{th}}(5)\sim 25\,\mathrm{keV}$.
Yet it remains one bit short of the 6-DoF anchor.
In this interface language, one may interpret quantum uncertainty as the coarse-lock deficit of mapping sub-geometric codes into a $3$D locality dictionary: the missing degree(s) of freedom appear as irreducible non-locality and probabilistic readout rather than as a determinate particle trajectory.

Although the technical core of this paper is anchored at the minimal balanced instance $(m,n)=(6,3)$, the $3$D rigid-frame coarse-lock dictionary induces a natural interface partition of the resolution axis into three regimes:
the sub-geometric vacuum ($m<6$), the geometric ground state ($m=6$), and hyper-geometric layers ($m>6$).

\subsection{Methodological note: auditable layering}
\label{subsec:audit_layering}

We follow an auditable separation between two layers:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Mathematical layer.}
Definitions and provable finite-resolution statements (counts, explicit maps, and rigid histograms).
\item \textbf{Physical identification layer.}
Mapping hypotheses and interface principles stated in a falsifiable form (no physical identification is used as a premise for the mathematical layer).
\end{itemize}
\paragraph{No-hidden-knobs contract (reader-facing).}
To reduce post-hoc flexibility, the paper follows a strict audit contract: whenever a quantitative claim is reported, it is either (i) a theorem-level finite statement, or (ii) an interface-level statement whose remaining freedom is restricted to an explicitly declared finite candidate family with deterministic tie-break rules (Definition~\ref{def:bounded_complexity_closure}), together with generated audit tables (domain sizes, gaps/quantiles, robustness, counterfactual baselines; Appendix~\ref{app:generated_tables} and Appendix~\ref{app:reproducibility}).
External reference targets (PDG/CODATA) enter only as comparison inputs; they are not fit parameters.
When within-family frequencies such as $1/|\Theta|$ are mentioned, they are interpreted as \emph{within the stated family} look-elsewhere context, not as a universal significance claim across unrestricted expression classes (see Section~\ref{sec:limitations_related_work}).
Across different reported closures, hypothesis classes share conventions and inputs and are therefore not statistically independent; we avoid multiplying within-family frequencies and use only conservative multiple-comparison bounds (e.g.\ Bonferroni) when summarizing several audited selections at once.

\subsection{Research questions and success criteria}

\subsection{Audit protocol: bounded-complexity closure and rigidity certificates}
\label{subsec:bounded_complexity_audit}

Several quantitative interface statements in this paper take the form of a \emph{bounded-complexity closure}:
we specify a small, discrete candidate family for a target observable and select a unique minimizer by a deterministic rule under an explicit finite complexity bound.
This is designed to address a standard audit concern in speculative constant/parameter matching: post-hoc freedom.

\begin{axiom}[Computational Action Principle (CAP; interface)]\label{ax:cap}
Within a fixed protocol class, realized effective structures are selected by minimizing accumulated readout mismatch (discrepancy) and implementation overhead subject to protocol constraints.
In the finite audited setting of this paper, CAP is instantiated by explicit bounded candidate families and deterministic selection rules: given a declared finite complexity budget and an explicit objective measuring mismatch to reference targets, select the unique minimizer with a fully specified tie-break rule (Definition~\ref{def:bounded_complexity_closure}).
\end{axiom}

\begin{definition}[Bounded-complexity closure (audit form)]\label{def:bounded_complexity_closure}
Fix reference targets $x_i^{\mathrm{ref}}>0$ and a candidate family $x_i(\theta)>0$ indexed by discrete parameters $\theta$.
For a bound $B\in\NN$, let $\Theta(B)$ be a finite domain of admissible parameters (the ``complexity box'').
Define the log-mismatch vector
\[
e_i(\theta):=\log\!\left(\frac{x_i(\theta)}{x_i^{\mathrm{ref}}}\right),
\]
and the summary objectives
\[
E_\infty(\theta):=\max_i |e_i(\theta)|,\qquad E_1(\theta):=\sum_i |e_i(\theta)|.
\]
A bounded-complexity closure is the selection of a unique $\theta_B\in\Theta(B)$ by lexicographic minimization:
first minimize $E_\infty$, then $E_1$, then any stated secondary criteria (e.g.\ coefficient sum), with a fully specified tie-break rule.
\end{definition}
\begin{remark}[A reusable audit template]\label{rem:cap_audit_template_pointer}
Appendix~\ref{app:cap_audit_template} records a compact deterministic template (including a reference pseudocode sketch) for the bounded-complexity closure rule used throughout the paper.
\end{remark}
\begin{remark}[Why log-mismatch is used]\label{rem:log_mismatch}
The log mismatch is dimensionless and symmetric under inversion: $\log(x/x^{\mathrm{ref}})=-\log(x^{\mathrm{ref}}/x)$.
It also linearizes multiplicative matching factors: if $x=s\,x^{\mathrm{ref}}$, then $\log(x/x^{\mathrm{ref}})=\log s$.
For small deviations, $|\log(x/x^{\mathrm{ref}})|\approx |x-x^{\mathrm{ref}}|/x^{\mathrm{ref}}$.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}[Rigidity certificate]\label{def:rigidity_certificate}
A closure is called \emph{rigid} on a tested range $B\in\{1,\dots,B_{\max}\}$ if the minimizer is unique at each $B$ and stabilizes:
there exists $B_\ast\le B_{\max}$ such that $\theta_B=\theta_{B_\ast}$ for all $B_\ast\le B\le B_{\max}$.
We record such stabilization tables when available (e.g.\ Table~\ref{tab:mass_depth_rigidity} and Table~\ref{tab:ckm_mixing_rigidity}).
\end{definition}

\paragraph{Audit outputs beyond point estimates.}
To reduce look-elsewhere and post-hoc flexibility, for each bounded-complexity closure we also record auditable auxiliary statistics:
the candidate-domain size $|\Theta|$, best/second-best gaps, and (for large domains) distribution quantiles (Table~\ref{tab:audit_closure_metrics} and Table~\ref{tab:audit_closure_quantiles}).
We additionally report minimizer stability rates under explicit target-perturbation models (Table~\ref{tab:audit_uncertainty_robustness}) and counterfactual baseline comparisons (Table~\ref{tab:audit_counterfactual}).
These tables are generated by deterministic scripts and are part of the paper's reproducibility contract (Appendix~\ref{app:reproducibility}).

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{$\ZZ_{128}$}{Z128} as a phase-register label}
\label{subsec:z128_label}

We model phases by a finite dyadic register $\ZZ_{2^p}$ as a protocol parameter, rather than by a continuous angle.
At the minimal window $m=6$, a natural baseline is a one-bit-finer phase register $p=m+1=7$, i.e.\ $\ZZ_{128}$.
In the concrete holonomy diagnostics of Section~\ref{sec:protocol_connections_holonomy}, the default phase embedding uses $\mathrm{denom}=2^m=64$, which is equivalently a $\ZZ_{128}$ register restricted to even residues since $2\pi\,\tau(k)/64=2\pi\,(2\tau(k))/128$.
Importantly, $p$ is treated as an explicit refinement knob: we report bounded sweeps over $\mathrm{denom}=2^p$ (e.g.\ Table~\ref{tab:holonomy_phase_lift_family} and Tables~\ref{tab:holonomy_perm_fit_pmns}--\ref{tab:holonomy_perm_fit_ckm}), so the role of dyadic phase resolution is auditable rather than assumed.
This is consistent with the stance adopted here that physical readout is fundamentally finite-resolution (Axiom~\ref{ax:readout_sequentiality} and Section~\ref{sec:hpa_readout}).

\begin{remark}[Avoiding a common confusion]\label{rem:z128_vs_omega6}
The label $\ZZ_{128}$ refers to the \emph{ontic phase register} alphabet (a finite ring model for phase arithmetic).
By contrast, $\Omega_6=\{0,1\}^6$ is the \emph{phenomenal readout window} alphabet produced after window projection and binarization.
These belong to different layers: a single phase register can induce multiple binary readout features, and the minimal balanced coupling choice $(m,n)=(6,3)$ on the chosen $2$D Hilbert screen is about the readout window length and Hilbert order, not about the size of the phase alphabet.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Main interface proposals}
\label{subsec:intro_contrib}

Building on the provable $64\to 21$ folding core, we propose three interface statements:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Gauge fields as compensating connections.}
When stable types are embedded on a Hilbert-addressed grid and cross-site readout consistency is required, local mismatch must be absorbed by compensating connections (Proposition~\ref{ax:gauge_compensation}).
The gauge-factor triple is recorded as a CAP-minimal interface closure (Proposition~\ref{prop:channel_to_gauge}; Section~\ref{subsec:gauge_as_compensation}).
\item \textbf{Chirality as protocol selection.}
Hilbert addressing is not unique: it comes in $D_4$ layout families, split into orientation-preserving and orientation-reversing classes \cite{Hilbert1891,Sagan1994}.
Fixing a layout class at $t=0$ turns parity into a protocol change and provides a readout-geometric mechanism for maximal parity violation in the weak sector (Section~\ref{sec:chirality_antimatter}).
\item \textbf{Antimatter as conjugate readout.}
Phase conjugation of the scan orbit is equivalent to scan reversal up to a phase shift; thus charge conjugation appears as a conjugate protocol and is tied to the same chirality sign datum at finite resolution (Section~\ref{sec:chirality_antimatter}).
\end{itemize}
