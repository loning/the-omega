\subsection{The Illusion of Volume: From Voxels to Textures}

In intuitive physical pictures, we tend to think that space is built from countless tiny \textbf{Voxels} stacked into a solid body. According to this view, a cubic space with side length $L$ should contain independent degrees of freedom (i.e., total information $I$) proportional to its volume:

\begin{equation}
I \propto L^3
\end{equation}

This is the \textbf{Volume Law}, and also the default assumption of classical field theory and fluid dynamics.

However, in computational science, this storage method is extremely inefficient. If the universe stores a $1 \text{cm}^3$ space at Planck resolution ($l_P \approx 10^{-35}$ meters), it would require approximately $10^{105}$ bits of data. Such a massive amount of data would be a heavy burden even for a universe-level computer.

The holographic principle tells us that nature adopts another encoding scheme. For any causally closed region, its effective degrees of freedom $N$ are strictly limited by the boundary surface area $A$:

\begin{equation}
N \le \frac{A}{4l_P^2} \propto L^2
\end{equation}

This means that when we delve into microscopic scales, the so-called "bulk space" does not provide additional information storage bits.

\textbf{Computational Implication}:

Three-dimensional space is not "solid" inside. It is more like a hollow balloon, with all physical information (particle positions, momenta, spins) actually encoded on the balloon's surface (boundary). Any point $(x, y, z)$ inside is not an independent storage unit, but a projection generated from boundary data $(u, v)$ through some complex \textbf{Non-local Mapping}. The "depth" we perceive is a manifestation of data correlation, not storage stacking.

\begin{equation}
C_{max} = \frac{\text{Area}}{4 \ln 2 \cdot l_P^2} \text{ bits}

The horizon's role is to "shield" excess information outside the causally connected region, ensuring that the effective information seen by external observers never exceeds the holographic bound.
\end{enumerate}
