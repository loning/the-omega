\section{Introduction}

A central tension in modern physics is that fundamental dynamics are typically expressed as reversible unitary evolution or continuous field equations, while empirical observations present themselves as discrete results, finite-precision statistics, and stable ``reified'' structures. This paper adopts a methodological inversion: rather than taking continuous spacetime and particles as primary ontology, it views them as observable projections of a static information structure under finite-resolution readout protocols. The purpose of this inversion is not to deny the validity of standard theories, but to provide an auditable chain of dependencies such that ``temporal sequence,'' ``spatial representation,'' and ``material stability'' acquire a unified protocol interpretation within a single formal framework.

The core ideas of this paper can be summarized in three points:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Static high-dimensional ontology:} A six-dimensional integer lattice serves as the phase--index backbone, hosting a static global state; no external time parameter is presupposed at the ontological layer.
  \item \textbf{Irrational-orientation cut-and-project:} Observation is equivalent to selecting a three-dimensional observable subspace and its complementary acceptance window, performing cut-and-project on the lattice point set to obtain three-dimensional quasi-periodic structure; its aperiodicity derives from irrational orientation.
  \item \textbf{Scan readout and stable sectors:} Temporal sequence is defined as discrete scan iterations of the complementary phase parameters; ``materialized information'' is defined as the type set that remains stable under finite-window readout after filtering through constrained channels; ``future'' is defined as the protocol-inaccessible portion of potential degrees of freedom outside the window.
\end{enumerate}

These three points constitute a structure that is formalizable, computable, and testable. This paper strictly distinguishes two classes of statements:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Mathematical layer:} Definitions, counting, mappings, and properties provable under given axioms;
  \item \textbf{Physical identification layer:} Interface hypotheses mapping mathematical objects to empirical objects (particles, forces, temporal arrow, etc.), which must be stated in a falsifiable manner and cannot be retroactively used as premises for the mathematical layer.
\end{itemize}
