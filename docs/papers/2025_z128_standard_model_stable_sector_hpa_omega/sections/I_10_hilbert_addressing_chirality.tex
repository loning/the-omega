
\section{Hilbert addressing and dihedral layout families}

\subsection{Hilbert addressing as a locality-preserving embedding}
\label{subsec:hilbert_map}

Under the linear-ontology interface of this paper (Axiom~\ref{ax:readout_sequentiality}), the primitive object is a one-dimensional scan order (tick stream).
To represent locality at finite resolution, we introduce \emph{space} as a derived display structure: an addressing basis folds a finite tick prefix into a neighborhood graph (Section~\ref{sec:tick_calculus}).
Hilbert addressing provides a classical locality-preserving bijection
\[
H_n:\{0,1,\dots,4^n-1\}\to \{0,1,\dots,2^n-1\}^2
\]
with bounded jump statistics relative to the Euclidean metric; see \cite{Hilbert1891,Sagan1994,MoonJagadishFaloutsosSaltz2001}.
At $n=3$, the grid has $4^3=64$ sites, matching the $64$ microstates of $\Omega_6$ for the chosen $2$D balanced coupling $(m,n)=(6,3)$.

\paragraph{A unified visual encoding of order and direction (used throughout).}
Whenever we draw finite-$n$ screens in this paper (2D Hilbert panels, 3D screens, fractal screens, or boundary-face projections), we overlay the same readout-order cue: a faint polyline connecting consecutive indices $k\mapsto k+1$ together with a monotone color gradient.
This is not an extra assumption; it is the explicit visualization of the underlying 1D scan order (Axiom~\ref{ax:readout_sequentiality}) under a declared addressing basis.
Accordingly, any apparent crossings or overlaps in a single 2D projection are \emph{display artifacts} (hidden coordinates discarded) and do not identify distinct microstates.

\subsubsection{Space from ticks: display graph, distance, and velocity (dictionary)}
\label{subsubsec:space_from_ticks_dictionary}

Fix an order $n$ and view tick indices as embedded on the grid by $H_n$.
We treat the resulting nearest-neighbor grid graph as the \emph{display graph} and use its graph metric as the protocol notion of distance (Definition~\ref{def:protocol_distance}).
Accordingly, a tick-indexed trajectory $x(t)$ on the display graph has a protocol velocity in sites per tick given by
\[
v(t_1,t_2)=\frac{d_n(x(t_1),x(t_2))}{|t_2-t_1|},
\]
as in Definition~\ref{def:protocol_velocity}.
Any conversion of this dimensionless rate to physical units, and therefore any identification with the measured constant $c$, is a matching-layer calibration (Remark~\ref{rem:scan_rate_c_dictionary}).

\subsubsection{Time hierarchy above ticks: tick-time, delay-time, and scale-time (dictionary)}
\label{subsubsec:time_hierarchy_above_ticks_dictionary}

\paragraph{Tick-time (ontic order).}
\InterfaceTag The primitive time parameter of the protocol is the tick index $t$ itself (Axiom~\ref{ax:readout_sequentiality}).
All finite observables are ultimately functions of finite tick windows (Appendix~\ref{app:tick_cap_derivation}), so ``time'' enters first as a strict linear order on readout updates.

\paragraph{Delay-time (operational time).}
\InterfaceTag A second, more structured notion of time arises when the tick stream is interpreted through a response/transport interface: delays, dwell times, and queue/backlog proxies are time observables defined \emph{on top of} tick-time (Section~\ref{sec:mass_latency_coordinate}; Appendix~\ref{app:k4_delay_audit}).
In this stance, ``measured time'' is a protocol-level dictionary: it is not assumed a priori but is evaluated from explicit finite channel registries and bounded mapping families.

\paragraph{Scale-time / RG-time (coarse-grained time).}
\InterfaceTag A third notion of time is induced by resolution and coarse graining.
Changing the window length $m$ (or, more generally, applying a declared coarse-graining map) changes which information can be stably resolved at fixed tick budget.
Accordingly, the resolution coordinate (e.g.\ $m$ itself, or the Fibonacci resolution coordinate $r$ used in the matching layer) acts as a \emph{scale-time} that orders effective descriptions.
In particular, RG equations in $\log\mu$ translate to linear flow in $r$ by Proposition~\ref{prop:rg_dictionary_r}.

\paragraph{Relation (one stream, multiple clocks).}
\AuditTag These time notions are related but not identical.
Tick-time is the ontic scan order; delay-time is an interface observable defined from responses over tick-time; scale-time is the coarse-graining order that reorganizes the same tick-indexed data into effective theories.
The lattice-QFT bridge in Section~\ref{subsec:hilbert_screen_lattice_qft_bridge} uses this separation explicitly: it fixes a finite spatial carrier (the Hilbert screen) and builds Gaussian kernels/propagators at fixed $(m,n)$, while renormalization conventions are treated as matching-layer choices recorded by explicit scale coordinates.

\paragraph{Wormhole-like shortcut channels (protocol-only).}
\InterfaceTag The paper's wormhole vocabulary is likewise fixed at the readout/interface layer.
The only concrete wormhole-like object defined in this manuscript is a pointer-jump shortcut edge on the Hilbert-indexed screen (Definition~\ref{def:wormhole_pointer_jump}), which explicitly relaxes scan-adjacency by augmenting the readout graph with a nonlocal pointer.
Accordingly, any shortcut/tunneling narrative in this paper is to be read as a controlled modification of \emph{readout geometry} (shortest paths and effective propagation routes) rather than as a premise about a traversable Lorentzian wormhole in vacuum GR (Appendix~\ref{app:bh_wormholes_pointer}).
When such shortcuts are used in response/propagation interface modules, they are always treated as paid channels subject to an explicit ledger and a wormhole-on/off counterfactual baseline (Section~\ref{sec:mass_spectrum_closure}).

\begin{remark}[Why the $2$D Hilbert grid is used in this paper]\label{rem:why_2d_hilbert}
Higher-dimensional locality-preserving space-filling curves (including $3$D Hilbert variants) exist.
Other $2$D space-filling curves can likewise be used as alternative addressing bases; we do not claim they fail.
At the minimal anchor used in this paper, the choice of addressing basis is made explicit and auditable by a bounded counterfactual comparison, in which Hilbert addressing is selected as CAP-minimal within the stated finite family (Table~\ref{tab:addressing_selection}).
Independently of this audit, Hilbert addressing provides a concrete locality-preserving representative with a fully explicit symmetry structure \cite{Sagan1994}.
In particular, one may use a $3$D Hilbert-type addressing map
\[
H_n^{(3)}:\{0,1,\dots,2^{3n}-1\}\to \{0,1,\dots,2^n-1\}^3,
\]
so that the order-$n$ hypercubic grid has $(2^n)^3=2^{3n}$ sites.
At window length $m=6$, the balanced cardinality match for $3$D addressing is $m=3n$, hence $n=2$ and $2^6=2^{3\cdot 2}=64$; equivalently, a $4\times 4\times 4$ grid carries the same $64$ sites as $\Omega_6$.
We use the classical $2$D Hilbert grid for three complementary reasons.
First, it is the minimal screen dimension that admits closed loops (plaquettes) and therefore supports the holonomy diagnostics used later (Section~\ref{sec:protocol_connections_holonomy}); in one dimension there are no cycles.
Second, it yields an explicit dihedral layout family with a transparent orientation-preserving vs.\ orientation-reversing split (Section~\ref{subsec:d4_family}), making the parity/chirality protocol audits fully explicit at the $64$-site anchor \cite{Sagan1994}.
Third, the $2$D grid can be read as a minimal finite ``screen'' on which the linear scan is rendered, consistent with a boundary-first (holographic) viewpoint in which observable data live on a readout boundary while bulk locality is an interface dictionary \cite{tHooft1993,Susskind1995,Bousso2002}.
Nothing in the folding statements depends on the addressing dimension; changing the addressing basis changes only which spatially organized diagnostics (e.g.\ chirality/holonomy histograms) are attached to the same theorem-level stable types.

\paragraph{A bounded counterfactual and a CAP-minimality audit.}
To make the addressing choice auditable in the tick-first language, we compare Hilbert addressing to a bounded counterfactual of equal description complexity: a row-major scan order on the same $8\times 8$ screen.
We score each candidate by protocol-internal locality metrics (no external targets): (i) high-quantile scan-path jump length (Manhattan distance) per tick, and (ii) neighbor-fiber matching overhead and a phase-lift computability diagnostic on nontrivial plaquettes.
Table~\ref{tab:addressing_selection} reports the resulting comparison and records the CAP-style deterministic selection within this explicit finite candidate family.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Addressing geometry versus ontic dimension]\label{rem:addressing_not_ontic_dimension}
The square grid and its Euclidean metric are used here as part of a protocol-level readout basis that makes locality auditable at finite resolution.
They do not serve as premises about an underlying ontic manifold; the theorem-level folding statements depend only on word combinatorics, while addressing enters only through controlled families of readout bases (Section~\ref{subsec:d4_family}).
\end{remark}

\subsection{The \texorpdfstring{$D_4$}{D4} layout family and non-canonicity}
\label{subsec:d4_family}

Hilbert recursion is not unique: there are $8$ global layout variants related by the dihedral group $D_4$ acting on the square by rotations and reflections.
These correspond to different address families (different orientation and reflection choices at recursion seed), and can be treated as different readout bases.
Rotation elements preserve orientation; reflection elements reverse orientation.

\begin{lemma}[$D_4$ layouts and orientation classes (standard)]\label{lem:d4_layouts}
The symmetry group of the square is the dihedral group $D_4$ with $|D_4|=8$ elements.
Composing a fixed order-$n$ Hilbert addressing map $H_n$ with any $g\in D_4$ yields another valid global layout with the same locality-preserving recursion statistics.
Moreover, $D_4$ splits into $4$ orientation-preserving rotations and $4$ orientation-reversing reflections.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The symmetry group of the square is classical and has $8$ elements.
Since each $g\in D_4$ is an isometry of the grid, the composition $g\circ H_n$ is again a bijection $\{0,\dots,4^n-1\}\to\{0,\dots,2^n-1\}^2$.
The rotation subgroup has order $4$ and preserves orientation; the remaining coset consists of reflections and reverses orientation \cite{Sagan1994}.
\end{proof}

This non-canonicity is the geometric entrance for chirality in the readout protocol:
fixing an orientation class at initialization selects a handedness for the address recursion.
In the physical identification layer, we elevate this to a \emph{protocol choice} whose mirror is not a symmetry within the same protocol (Section~\ref{sec:chirality_antimatter}).

\paragraph{Non-canonicity as protocol, not gauge.}
Changing Hilbert layout changes the address mapping and therefore changes certain finite statistics of neighborhood patterns.
At the same time, the folding core of Section~\ref{sec:folding_core} is protocol-stable: the $64\to 21$ and $18\oplus 3$ statements are invariant as combinatorics of words, independent of the spatial embedding.
The role of $D_4$ is therefore not to modify the folding theorem, but to provide a controlled family of readout bases in which chirality can be defined and tested.

\subsection{A discrete Hilbert chirality index}
\label{subsec:hilbert_chi}

Let $p_0,p_1,\dots,p_{4^n-1}\in\ZZ^2$ be the Hilbert path points, with $p_k=H_n(k)$.
Define the discrete chirality index
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hilbert_chi_def}
\chi_H:=\sum_{k=1}^{4^n-2}\mathrm{sgn}\!\left( (p_k-p_{k-1})\times (p_{k+1}-p_k)\right),
\end{equation}
where the $2$D cross product is the scalar $ (a_x,a_y)\times(b_x,b_y):=a_x b_y-a_y b_x$ and $\mathrm{sgn}(t)\in\{-1,0,+1\}$.
Intuitively, $\chi_H$ is the net excess of left turns over right turns along the discrete path.
We take $\mathrm{sgn}(0)=0$, so collinear triples contribute $0$; thus $\chi_H$ counts signed turns and ignores straight steps.

\begin{proposition}[Parity and traversal reversal flip $\chi_H$]\label{prop:chi_flip}
Let $P$ be any reflection of the grid and let $T$ be traversal reversal (path reversal).
Then
\[
\chi_H(P\cdot p)=-\chi_H(p),\qquad
\chi_H(T\cdot p)=-\chi_H(p),
\]
while orientation-preserving rigid motions (rotations and translations) preserve $\chi_H$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Reflections reverse orientation, hence reverse the sign of the scalar cross product at every non-collinear local turn.
Traversal reversal swaps the order of each local triple $(p_{k-1},p_k,p_{k+1})$, which also flips the cross-product sign.
Rotations preserve orientation and therefore preserve cross-product signs.
Translations cancel in the differences $p_k-p_{k-1}$ and $p_{k+1}-p_k$, hence leave every cross product unchanged.
\end{proof}

Appendix~\ref{app:generated_tables} records a reproducible $n=3$ check (generated by \texttt{scripts/exp\_hilbert\_chirality\_index.py}): the canonical Hilbert path has $\chi_H=-2$, while both reflection and traversal reversal yield $\chi_H=+2$.
