\section{Tick calculus: deriving observables, space, gauge, and scale from sequential readout}
\label{sec:tick_calculus}

\paragraph{Aim.}
This section fixes the time-first spine used throughout the paper.
We treat the tick (scan iteration count) as the only primitive input and define all physical-language quantities as derived protocol structures.
Mathematical-layer statements (counts, maps, and finite tables) remain as in Sections~\ref{sec:hpa_readout} and \ref{sec:folding_core}; the additional content here is a dictionary that maps each physical concept to an explicit mathematical object.

\subsection{The tick as the only primitive input}
\label{subsec:tick_only_input}

\begin{definition}[Tick (scan time)]
\label{def:tick}
The \emph{tick} is the scan iteration index $t\in\ZZ$.
The direction $t\mapsto t+1$ is the operational notion of forward time within the protocol.
\end{definition}

\paragraph{Status.}
Definition~\ref{def:tick} is not an additional dynamical assumption; it is a notational declaration of what is meant by time in a scan-first ontology (Axiom~\ref{ax:readout_sequentiality}).

\subsection{Time orientation and initialization: the arrow of time in tick-only language}
\label{subsec:tick_time_arrow}

\paragraph{Orientation is part of sequential execution.}
At the mathematical layer, the scan orbit can be indexed by $\ZZ$ and is formally symmetric under reversal $t\mapsto -t$.
In the physical identification layer of this paper, however, ``time'' is the \emph{operational} ordering of updates in the readout protocol (Definition~\ref{def:tick}), so the direction $t\mapsto t+1$ is the forward execution direction by definition.
This is not a new physical input: it is the meaning of ``running'' the protocol rather than specifying a static mathematical object.

\paragraph{Origin choice and the Big Bang as a protocol start.}
Only tick differences are used in the protocol observables (e.g.\ velocities use $|t_2-t_1|$ in Definition~\ref{def:protocol_velocity}), so shifting the origin $t\mapsto t+t_0$ is a coordinate convention whenever one is not probing a boundary condition.
In the tick-only picture, ``Big Bang'' is the protocol-level statement that the tick counter has a start event; we denote that global initialization by choosing an origin $t=0$ and regarding the physically instantiated history as the half-line $t\ge 0$.
Throughout the paper we keep $t\in\ZZ$ for algebraic convenience, but interpret the origin as the unique global start of the executed run when cosmological language is used.

\begin{definition}[Local observer clock (birth as a local origin)]
\label{def:local_observer_clock}
Fix a global tick coordinate $t$ and a local initialization tick $t_\ast\in\ZZ$ at which an observer's record begins (``birth'' in protocol language).
The observer's intrinsic tick coordinate is the translated clock
\[
t_{\mathrm{obs}}:=t-t_\ast\in\ZZ.
\]
The observer's forward direction is $t_{\mathrm{obs}}\mapsto t_{\mathrm{obs}}+1$, inherited from $t\mapsto t+1$.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[Big Bang arrow versus observer arrow]
\label{rem:bang_equals_birth_arrow}
In tick-only language, both the Big Bang and an observer's birth are instances of the same mathematical operation: selecting an origin on the tick line.
A global start fixes the universe-wide origin $t=0$, while an observer start fixes a local origin $t_{\mathrm{obs}}=0$ (Definition~\ref{def:local_observer_clock}).
Up to the translation gauge $t\mapsto t+t_0$, the two are equivalent: they carry the same oriented tick direction and differ only by an offset.
If the protocol were to admit domains initialized with different orientation data (cf.\ SCL for the addressing orientation class, Assumption~\ref{ax:scl}), then parity-/time-odd sign data tied to protocol orientation would flip across such domains; see the domain-wall test language in Prediction P2.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Irreversibility from finite observability (no new axiom).}
The arrow of time in this paper is not postulated as an extra dynamical law.
It arises from the fact that what is called ``physics'' here is not the full microscopic state but the finite record obtained by window projection (Section~\ref{subsec:tick_to_words}) and subsequent stability folding (Section~\ref{subsec:tick_to_stability}), both of which are many-to-one.
Consequently, the observable word history does not determine a unique microstate history, and reversal at the observable level corresponds to a different protocol (e.g.\ traversal reversal together with a matched window/conjugation dictionary; Section~\ref{subsec:ptc_definitions}).

\subsection{From ticks to finite observables: windows and words}
\label{subsec:tick_to_words}

\paragraph{Observable records are finite.}
Fix a window length $m\ge 1$.
Finite readout is represented by length-$m$ binary words
\[
\Omega_m:=\{0,1\}^m,
\qquad |\Omega_m|=2^m,
\]
obtained by window projection of the scan stream (Section~\ref{subsec:window_projection}).
The elementary observational record at resolution $m$ is therefore a pair \emph{(tick, word)}:
the tick $t$ locates the sample in the ordered stream, while the word encodes what was seen through the chosen windowing predicate.

\subsection{Stability as an intrinsic grammar: admissible types and folding}
\label{subsec:tick_to_stability}

\paragraph{Stability is a predicate on words.}
At the golden branch, the $\varphi$-channel selects the admissible set $X_m\subset\Omega_m$ by a forbidden-word grammar (no adjacent ones), and one has the Fibonacci count $|X_m|=F_{m+2}$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:xm_fib}).
The $\pi$-channel induces the cyclic/boundary split (Proposition~\ref{prop:cyc_bdry_size}).

\paragraph{Microstates and projection.}
At fixed $m$, the protocol distinguishes:
\begin{itemize}
\item microstate labels in $\Omega_m$ (what can appear at the raw window level), and
\item stable type labels in $X_m$ (what survives the stability predicate).
\end{itemize}
The folding map $\mathrm{Fold}_m$ (Definition~\eqref{eq:foldm_def}) is the deterministic projection from the $2^m$ raw indices to the $|X_m|$ stable types; it is surjective for all $m$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:foldm_surjective}).

\subsection{Space as addressing: display graphs, distance, and velocity}
\label{subsec:tick_to_space}

\paragraph{Space is a derived display structure.}
To speak about locality, the protocol must choose an addressing basis that embeds a finite tick prefix into a neighborhood graph.
At Hilbert order $n$, the canonical finite prefix is $t\in\{0,\dots,4^n-1\}$ and the Hilbert addressing map is a bijection
\[
H_n:\{0,1,\dots,4^n-1\}\to \{0,1,\dots,2^n-1\}^2
\]
(Section~\ref{sec:hilbert_addressing}).

\begin{definition}[Addressing map and display graph]
\label{def:addressing_map_graph}
Fix an order $n$ and an addressing map $A_n$ from indices to sites.
For the Hilbert screen, $A_n:=H_n$.
Define the \emph{display graph} $G_n$ as the nearest-neighbor graph on the site set $A_n(\{0,\dots,4^n-1\})$, with edges given by unit Manhattan adjacency on the grid.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Protocol distance]
\label{def:protocol_distance}
For sites $x,y$ in the display graph $G_n$, define the protocol distance
\[
d_n(x,y):=\text{shortest-path distance between $x$ and $y$ in $G_n$}.
\]
\end{definition}

\paragraph{Tick-distance relation.}
Definition~\ref{def:protocol_distance} makes the slogan ``distance is time'' precise at the protocol level:
distance is the minimal number of adjacency moves needed to compare or transport readout between two displayed sites, hence a tick-count proxy once a per-move tick budget is fixed.

\begin{definition}[Protocol velocity and maximal signal rate (tick units)]
\label{def:protocol_velocity}
Given a tick-indexed site trajectory $x(t)\in G_n$, define the protocol velocity (in sites per tick) by
\[
v(t_1,t_2):=\frac{d_n(x(t_1),x(t_2))}{|t_2-t_1|}\qquad (t_1\neq t_2).
\]
The maximal protocol signal rate on $G_n$ is the supremum of $v$ over admissible update rules.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[Physical units and the speed-of-light dictionary]
\label{rem:tick_velocity_to_c}
The quantities in Definitions~\ref{def:protocol_distance} and \ref{def:protocol_velocity} are dimensionless (graph units per tick).
Any identification with meters/seconds, and hence any identification with the measured constant $c$, is a matching-layer calibration (Remark~\ref{rem:scan_rate_c_dictionary}).
\end{remark}

\subsection{Gauge data as fiber compensation}
\label{subsec:tick_to_gauge}

\paragraph{Why connections are forced.}
At fixed $m$, stable labels $w\in X_m$ carry finite microstate fibers $P(w)=\mathrm{Fold}_m^{-1}(w)$.
Once the protocol demands cross-site consistency on the display graph, stable labels alone are insufficient: one must also specify how fibers are matched between neighbors (Proposition~\ref{ax:gauge_compensation}).
This is the finite origin of compensating connection data and gauge redundancy.

\subsection{Overhead, delay, and scale: mass and energy as time dictionaries}
\label{subsec:tick_to_mass_energy}

\paragraph{Overhead as time-lag.}
In a tick-first ontology, ``mass as depth'' is an operational statement about overhead: stabilizing a pattern consumes additional local protocol resources.
At the matching layer, this overhead can be accessed through delay observables (Wigner--Smith; Appendix~\ref{app:time_mass_delay}) and compared to clock-rate/lapse dictionaries.

\paragraph{A single log-time coordinate.}
Fix the electron reference $\mu_0=m_e$ and define the resolution coordinate
\[
r(\mu):=\frac{\log(\mu/m_e)}{\log\varphi}.
\]
By the Compton-clock dictionary (Appendix~\ref{app:time_mass_delay}), $r$ is simultaneously a log-frequency and (up to a sign) a log-time coordinate.
This is the sense in which mass and energy are derived from time in the present protocol language: they are calibrated re-expressions of time-scale ratios relative to the $m=6$ anchor.
