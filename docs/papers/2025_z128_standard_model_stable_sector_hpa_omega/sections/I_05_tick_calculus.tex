\section{Tick calculus: deriving observables, space, gauge, and scale from sequential readout}
\label{sec:tick_calculus}

\paragraph{Aim.}
This section fixes the time-first spine used throughout the paper.
We treat the tick (scan iteration count) as the only primitive input and CAP as the only primitive closure/selection rule, and we define all physical-language quantities as derived protocol structures.
Mathematical-layer statements (counts, maps, and finite tables) remain as in Sections~\ref{sec:hpa_readout} and \ref{sec:folding_core}; the additional content here is a dictionary that maps each physical concept to an explicit mathematical object.

\subsection{The tick as the only primitive input}
\label{subsec:tick_only_input}

\begin{definition}[Tick (scan time)]
\label{def:tick}
The \emph{tick} is the scan iteration index $t\in\ZZ$.
The direction $t\mapsto t+1$ is the operational notion of forward time within the protocol.
\end{definition}

\subsection{Time orientation and initialization: the arrow of time in tick-only language}
\label{subsec:tick_time_arrow}

\paragraph{Orientation is part of sequential execution.}
\InterfaceTag At the mathematical layer, the scan orbit can be indexed by $\ZZ$ and is formally symmetric under reversal $t\mapsto -t$.
In the executed protocol, ``time'' is the operational update order (Definition~\ref{def:tick}), so $t\mapsto t+1$ is the forward direction by definition.

\paragraph{Origin choice.}
\InterfaceTag Protocol observables depend only on tick differences (e.g.\ Definition~\ref{def:protocol_velocity}), so shifting $t\mapsto t+t_0$ is a coordinate convention away from boundary conditions.

\paragraph{Irreversibility from finite observability (no new axiom).}
\InterfaceTag The arrow is not postulated as an extra dynamical law: it arises because ``physics'' here is the finite record after window projection and stability folding, both many-to-one.
Consequently, observable word histories are not invertible to unique microstate histories, and reversal corresponds to a different protocol/run (Section~\ref{subsec:ptc_definitions}).

\subsection{From ticks to finite observables: windows and words}
\label{subsec:tick_to_words}

\paragraph{Observable records are finite.}
Fix a window length $m\ge 1$.
Finite readout is represented by length-$m$ binary words
\[
\Omega_m:=\{0,1\}^m,
\qquad |\Omega_m|=2^m,
\]
obtained by window projection of the scan stream (Section~\ref{subsec:window_projection}).
\MathTag At resolution $m$, an elementary record is the pair \emph{(tick, word)}.

\subsection{Stability as an intrinsic grammar: admissible types and folding}
\label{subsec:tick_to_stability}

\paragraph{Stability is a predicate on words.}
At the golden branch, the $\varphi$-channel selects the admissible set $X_m\subset\Omega_m$ by a forbidden-word grammar (no adjacent ones), and one has the Fibonacci count $|X_m|=F_{m+2}$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:xm_fib}).
The $\pi$-channel induces the cyclic/boundary split (Proposition~\ref{prop:cyc_bdry_size}).

\paragraph{Microstates and projection.}
\MathTag The folding map $\mathrm{Fold}_m$ (Definition~\eqref{eq:foldm_def}) deterministically projects microstate indices in $\Omega_m$ to stable types in $X_m$; it is surjective for all $m$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:foldm_surjective}).

\subsection{Space as addressing: display graphs, distance, and velocity}
\label{subsec:tick_to_space}

\paragraph{Space is a derived display structure.}
To speak about locality, the protocol must choose an addressing basis that embeds a finite tick prefix into a neighborhood graph.
At Hilbert order $n$, the canonical finite prefix is $t\in\{0,\dots,4^n-1\}$ and the Hilbert addressing map is a bijection
\[
H_n:\{0,1,\dots,4^n-1\}\to \{0,1,\dots,2^n-1\}^2
\]
(Section~\ref{sec:hilbert_addressing}).

\begin{definition}[Addressing map and display graph]
\label{def:addressing_map_graph}
Fix an order $n$ and an addressing map $A_n$ from indices to sites.
For the Hilbert screen, $A_n:=H_n$.
Define the \emph{display graph} $G_n$ as the nearest-neighbor graph on the site set $A_n(\{0,\dots,4^n-1\})$, with edges given by unit Manhattan adjacency on the grid.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Protocol distance]
\label{def:protocol_distance}
For sites $x,y$ in the display graph $G_n$, define the protocol distance
\[
d_n(x,y):=\text{shortest-path distance between $x$ and $y$ in $G_n$}.
\]
\end{definition}

\paragraph{Tick-distance relation.}
\InterfaceTag Distance is a tick-count proxy once a per-move tick budget is fixed (Definition~\ref{def:protocol_distance}).

\begin{definition}[Protocol velocity and maximal signal rate (tick units)]
\label{def:protocol_velocity}
Given a tick-indexed site trajectory $x(t)\in G_n$, define the protocol velocity (in sites per tick) by
\[
v(t_1,t_2):=\frac{d_n(x(t_1),x(t_2))}{|t_2-t_1|}\qquad (t_1\neq t_2).
\]
The maximal protocol signal rate on $G_n$ is the supremum of $v$ over admissible update rules.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[Physical units and the speed-of-light dictionary]
\label{rem:tick_velocity_to_c}
The quantities in Definitions~\ref{def:protocol_distance} and \ref{def:protocol_velocity} are dimensionless (graph units per tick).
Any identification with meters/seconds, and hence any identification with the measured constant $c$, is a matching-layer calibration (Remark~\ref{rem:scan_rate_c_dictionary}).
\end{remark}

\subsection{Bulk dimension as a CAP output from the anchor bit budget}
\label{subsec:tick_to_bulk_dimension}

\paragraph{Screen versus bulk.}
The addressing screen used for finite diagnostics is a protocol-level readout basis (Section~\ref{sec:hilbert_addressing}).
By contrast, the \emph{bulk dimension} $d$ is an interface parameter that enters when one asks what it means to \emph{display} a localized rigid object and to compare its pose across sites.
In the tick-only language, $d$ is not taken as a primitive input: it is selected from a finite candidate set by CAP at the chosen anchor scale.

\begin{definition}[Rigid-frame coarse-lock budget (tick-only interface)]
\label{def:rigid_frame_budget}
Let $d\ge 1$ be a candidate bulk dimension.
A local rigid frame has $d$ translational degrees of freedom and $d(d-1)/2$ rotational degrees of freedom, hence $\dim SE(d)=d(d+1)/2$.
Under the minimal nontrivial coarse-lock convention (two bins per independent parameter), a single binary window of length $m$ can coarse-lock a rigid frame only if
\[
m\;\ge\;\dim SE(d)=\frac{d(d+1)}{2}.
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[CAP-maximal bulk dimension at the anchor]\label{prop:bulk_dimension_from_anchor}
At the chosen anchor window length $m=6$, CAP selects the maximal bulk dimension compatible with the coarse-lock budget in Definition~\ref{def:rigid_frame_budget}, namely $d=3$.
Equivalently, $\dim SE(3)=6$ matches the anchor bit budget.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For $d=4$ one has $\dim SE(4)=10>6$, so a single $m=6$ window cannot coarse-lock a $4$D rigid frame under the minimal two-bin convention.
For $d=3$ one has $\dim SE(3)=6$, so $d=3$ is admissible and saturates the budget.
Thus the maximal admissible $d$ is $3$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Gauge data as fiber compensation}
\label{subsec:tick_to_gauge}

\paragraph{Why connections are forced.}
At fixed $m$, stable labels $w\in X_m$ carry finite microstate fibers $P(w)=\mathrm{Fold}_m^{-1}(w)$.
Once the protocol demands cross-site consistency on the display graph, stable labels alone are insufficient: one must also specify how fibers are matched between neighbors (Proposition~\ref{prop:gauge_compensation}).
This is the finite origin of compensating connection data and gauge redundancy.

\subsection{Overhead, delay, and scale: mass and energy as time dictionaries}
\label{subsec:tick_to_mass_energy}

\paragraph{Overhead as time-lag.}
In a tick-first ontology, ``mass as depth'' is an operational statement about overhead: stabilizing a pattern consumes additional local protocol resources.
At the matching layer, this overhead can be accessed through delay observables (Wigner--Smith; Appendix~\ref{app:time_mass_delay}) and compared to clock-rate/lapse dictionaries.

\paragraph{A single log-time coordinate.}
Fix the electron reference $\mu_0=m_e$ and define the resolution coordinate
\[
r(\mu):=\frac{\log(\mu/m_e)}{\log\varphi}.
\]
By the Compton-clock dictionary (Appendix~\ref{app:time_mass_delay}), $r$ is simultaneously a log-frequency and (up to a sign) a log-time coordinate.
This is the sense in which mass and energy are derived from time in the present protocol language: they are calibrated re-expressions of time-scale ratios relative to the $m=6$ anchor.
